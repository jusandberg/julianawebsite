Lesson 1 · What it is

Not what most people think.

Emotional intelligence isn't about being nice, and it isn't about hiding what you feel. Tap each card to check a common belief.

In one sentence: emotional intelligence is noticing what you feel, managing it, and using it to understand yourself, connect with others and make better decisions.

Five areas, one wheel.

Area 2 of 5

Self-expression.

Self-perception turned outward: putting feelings into words, speaking up, and standing on your own feet. People aren't left guessing where you stand.

The three skills

Emotional expressionAssertivenessIndependence
Try it · Three ways to say itA teammate keeps booking meetings over your focus time. Label each response.
"It's fine, I'll just work late again."
Passive. Your need goes unsaid, so nothing changes.
"You clearly don't respect anyone's time but your own."
Aggressive. It attacks the person and leaves out what you need.
"I need my focus blocks to hit our deadline. Can we find another time for these?"
Assertive. Clear about the need, respectful of the other person.
Carry this: assertive means clear about what you need and respectful of the other person, both at once.

Area 4 of 5

Decision making.

How you use emotion when you solve problems: seeing things as they really are, and not acting on impulse.

The three skills

Problem solvingReality testingImpulse control
Try it · Fact or story?At 4:55 pm your manager emails: "We need to talk tomorrow." Your mind starts racing. Sort each thought.
The email arrived at 4:55 pm.
Fact. You can check it.
I'm in trouble.
Story. It might be true, but you don't know yet.
It says "We need to talk tomorrow."
Fact. Those are the words on the screen.
She's unhappy with my work.
Story. Your mind filled the gap. Checking before reacting is reality testing.
Carry this: two facts, two stories. Good decisions start with telling them apart.

The goal

Explain emotional intelligence in one sentence, name the five areas of the EQ wheel, and choose one area to practise this week.

Why each activity looks the way it does

  1. Belief cards first. Most people arrive with a wrong idea of emotional intelligence. Clearing that up first makes room for the real definition.
  2. A wheel to explore. The model is fifteen skills in five areas. Letting learners open it piece by piece is easier to take in than a list or a lecture.
  3. One activity per area. Each activity uses a skill from that area: naming a feeling, speaking up, replying with empathy, telling fact from story, staying steady. Learners do the skill, not just read about it.
  4. Feedback on every choice. Wrong answers explain why they fall short, so a miss still teaches.
  5. A final check with new situations. Matching fresh moments to areas shows understanding, not memory of the lessons.
  6. Pick one area. The course ends with one small practice to try this week, so it leads to action.

How I would measure it

Level 1A one-question pulse: which area felt most useful to you, and why?
Level 2Score out of five on the final check, and which area was missed most across the group.
Level 3A two-week check-in: did you try your chosen practice, and what happened?
Level 4Over time, engagement and feedback scores for teams whose leaders finish the full program.
