Case study · Sample model

A manager development program that's connected to how people grow

Most leadership programs run on their own, separate from levels, reviews and promotions. This sample shows how I would design one that's tied into job architecture, performance, internal mobility and succession planning, so what managers learn shows up in how the company actually runs.

This is a sample model that shows how I'd approach the work. The levels, timelines and measures are examples, not results from a past program.

Who it's for
First-time managers, experienced managers and senior leaders
Format
Cohorts by level, about six months each, blended live and self-paced
Connected to
Job architecture, performance reviews, internal moves and succession
My role
Needs analysis, program architecture, design, stakeholder alignment and measurement
The problem

What usually goes wrong when leadership training stands alone

These are patterns I see across a lot of companies. A good model has to answer each one.

People get promoted for the wrong reasons

Strong individual contributors become managers without anyone defining what good management looks like at their level.

The training and the reviews don't match

Managers learn one set of skills in a workshop, then get reviewed on something else entirely.

Internal moves depend on who you know

Good people leave because they can't see a path, and open roles get filled from outside by default.

Succession plans live in a spreadsheet

Names go on a list once a year, but nobody is actually getting those people ready.

The model

How the five parts connect

The program sits in the middle. Job architecture defines what good looks like at each level, and performance, internal mobility and succession all use the same definitions. Choose a level, then select any part to see how it works.

How I'd build it

From the first conversations to the first cohort

I'd use SAM, the Successive Approximation Model, so leaders and HR partners react to something real early, and plan the measures before building anything.

  1. Weeks 1 to 6

    Discover

    Talk with managers, their leaders and HR partners. Review the job architecture, performance data, engagement results and exit themes. Agree on three to five business outcomes.

  2. Weeks 7 to 8

    Design together

    Run a working session with stakeholders to turn each level's expectations into program outcomes, then sketch the first module and the model.

    Based on a SAM Savvy Start. Try my SAM sample
  3. Weeks 9 to 20

    Prototype and pilot

    Build one module, test it with a small pilot cohort, and revise in quick rounds before building the rest.

  4. Month 6 onward

    Launch by level

    Open cohorts for each level. Each participant's own manager joins at the kickoff, the midpoint and the end.

  5. Every cohort

    Measure and improve

    Practice and feedback during the program, a skills check at the end, and talent measures reviewed twice a year.

    Formative and summative checks. Try my assessment sample
By level

What managers learn at each level

Each track is built from that level's expectations in the job architecture, and draws on proven leadership models.

Short self-paced lessonsFive-minute microlearning before each live session, so time together goes to practice.
Live practice labsSmall groups practise real conversations, like feedback, conflict and career talks.
Peer coaching circlesGroups of four to six meet monthly to work through real problems using GROW.
A stretch projectA real piece of work tied to the business, reviewed with the participant's leader.
Measuring it

What I would track, and when

The first two levels tell you whether the program works. The last two tell you whether it matters to the business, and those are the ones leaders care about.

ReactionA short pulse after each session: how useful was this for something you're dealing with now?Every session

What I'd watch for

The risks I would plan around from the start

Leaders who aren't involved. If participants' own managers don't take part, new habits fade within weeks. That's why they join at the kickoff, the midpoint and the end.

A job architecture that's too complicated. If people can't explain their own level in a sentence, it won't guide anything. I'd keep it to a few levels with plain descriptions.

Succession lists that feel secret. People should know they're being developed, even if the list itself stays private. Otherwise the best people still leave.

Measuring only attendance. Completion is easy to count and says little. I'd agree on the business measures with leaders before the first cohort starts.
